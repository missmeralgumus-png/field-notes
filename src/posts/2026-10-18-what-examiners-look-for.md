This guide is written for teachers who are preparing pilots or air traffic controllers for an ICAO language proficiency assessment. It is not a repetition of the rating scale, which you already have. It is a working account of what an examiner is actually listening for in the room, how levels show themselves in a candidate's speech, how an interlocutor is expected to behave, and how all of that should shape what you do in class. It draws on notes accumulated across years of examining, and it is written to be useful in lesson planning tomorrow morning.

### 1. The Question Behind Every Rating

Before any descriptor is consulted, every examiner is quietly asking themselves one question:

> Is this performance safe to fly? Would I get on an aircraft with this person at the controls, or working the position that separates it from other traffic?

The ICAO rating scale is the formal instrument, but this is the lens the rating scale is being read through. The six skills, the six levels, the descriptors — all of it exists to answer that one safety question in a consistent, defensible way. Everything in this guide, and everything you ask candidates to do in class, should ultimately prepare them to answer yes to it.

### 2. Who Is a Good Communicator in Aviation?

A good aviation communicator is not necessarily an elegant English speaker. From the examiner's desk, three qualities stand out.

- **They can describe what is around them, what is happening now, and what they think.** Their English is wide enough to put the operational picture into words — the surroundings, the present moment, their own ideas — without reaching for memorised formulas and without going silent when the formula does not fit.
- **They do not assume or interpret — they check.** When they are not sure they have understood correctly, they ask. They confirm. They clarify. A candidate who nods and guesses is a candidate who will one day read back something they did not understand.
- **They have the English they need to choose the right word, pronounce it clearly, and understand common and work-related topics with ease.** The scope is defined; the control within that scope has to be real.

A fourth, cross-cutting quality is organised thinking. Sophisticated language production is not about long words or complex tenses — it is about presenting thoughts and opinions in an organised way. Teach your learners to put a short answer together with a reason and an example. Three to four sentences, structured, is far more revealing than one long rambling turn or one short defensive one.

### 3. How Levels Show Themselves in the Room

The rating scale descriptors describe the *what*; what follows is the *feel* of each level as it unfolds in an actual assessment. Teach to the feel as well as to the words.

**Level 2 — Elementary.** Memorised sentences doing the work of real language. Pronunciation is heavily shaped by the first language. Communication is possible only on the most predictable themes, in the most familiar patterns, and collapses the moment a question is not what the candidate was rehearsing for.

**Level 3 — Pre-Operational.** A candidate at Level 3 is not a beginner. They have a real grasp of English — a potential Level 4 if work continues — but it is not yet enough for the radio. They tend to interpret the meaning of what they hear rather than process it precisely; answers are often incomplete; interaction is weak; ordering ideas is a struggle. One hard truth: if a candidate is a Level 3, it does not matter how many times you replay the audio — they will not get there on this attempt. The ceiling is in their processing, not in their listening effort.

**Level 4 — Operational (the minimum to fly).** Meaning is conveyed at all times. Basic control of sentence structure. Comprehension is good enough to cover what they must understand — the instructions and the questions. The communication bridge is open the whole way across. There are momentary glitches, stumbles, loss of smoothness — but the message gets through, you know what they mean, and they stay engaged. Level 4 is overall successful communication. It is not pretty; it is effective.

**Level 5 — Extended.** The candidate is now working the language, not just surviving it. Paraphrasing is comfortable. They can conclude, organise ideas, and even when they are grammatically wrong they are structurally ambitious — attempting complex structures and elaborate tenses that are well framed most of the time. Hesitations are small and few.

**Level 6 — Expert.** Sound, easy, sophisticated, effortless. Not necessarily native, but entirely at home in the language of the operational context and well beyond it.

**The 'rarely / sometimes / often' test.** When you are deciding which level a sample belongs to, keep three adverbs in mind. The descriptors across the levels pivot on how often something happens — does a feature rarely appear (characteristic of higher levels), sometimes appear (middle), or often appear (lower)? Train yourself and your learners to listen for frequency, not just presence. One missed word is not a Level 3; a pattern of missed words under pressure is.

### 4. What You Are Actually Being Asked to Teach

The exam does not test aviation knowledge. It tests how well a candidate communicates in English in an aviation context. That one sentence should reorganise your lesson planning.

Specifically, candidates must be able to:

- **Understand** — grasp the question or instruction accurately, and the complication when it comes.
- **Defend themselves linguistically** — keep talking, keep the channel open, when the material is unfamiliar.
- **Interact** — respond, build on, come back to the interlocutor; take turns rather than deliver monologues or go silent.
- **Listen first** — and act on what they heard rather than on what they expected to hear.
- **React to the unknown.** How does the candidate behave when they did not understand? This single moment is the most diagnostic of the whole assessment.

Interaction is the most important thing. Interaction, in examiner terms, means keeping the channel open. We are moving forward together. The candidate who asks for clarification is not failing — they are demonstrating a Level-4-and-above behaviour. The candidate who stays silent, or who read-backs a guess, is the one in trouble. Teach this openly: before the exam, remind your learners that if they do not understand, they have to ask.

Train for enough speech. Candidates sometimes think short answers are safer. They are not. If the sample is too thin, there is nothing on which to award a higher level. Three or four sentences, with a reason and an example, is the target length for an answer — enough to show the examiner what the candidate can do.

### 5. The Interlocutor's Role (and Why Teachers Should Learn It)

Even if you never sit as a formal interlocutor, every mock exam you run in class puts you in that role. The interlocutor's job is not to teach, correct, or help — it is to elicit a speech sample under conditions as close as possible to the real test. The principles are worth absorbing.

**What the interlocutor does:**

- Guides the candidate through the script, keeping it short and simple, and giving the candidate the space to produce language.
- Remains in the background and stays neutral. You are an observer, a good guide. You are there for the candidate, so using their name is appropriate; being offensive or patronising is not.
- Asks open-ended questions. Yes/no prompts rarely elicit a ratable sample.
- Processes vocabulary, communication and interaction in parallel as the sample unfolds — you are not just waiting for a chance to speak; you are already tracking what you are hearing against the scale.
- Paces the exchange to the candidate. Slower for weaker candidates. Faster, closer to real radiotelephony speed, for stronger ones.
- Equalises the data. A shy candidate should be asked the kind of questions a chatty candidate would answer unprompted, so that you leave the exam with a comparable sample for both.

**What the interlocutor does not do:**

- Does not help. If the candidate pauses, do not supply the word. Wait. If the pause lengthens, ask "Do you understand?" or "Do you have an answer?" but do not rescue them — the pause itself is data.
- Does not elaborate when it is not necessary. You prompt; you do not perform.
- Does not condescend, patronise, lecture or show impatience. How smooth and respectful the test feels is itself a quality marker of the examining.
- Does not accept visual feedback as communication. Mimicking, nodding, a hand gesture — these do not count toward a language sample. If the candidate relies on them, you clarify verbally and bring the exchange back into words.
- Does not mix in opinions on the pictures. In picture tasks, teach candidates to describe the photo objectively: what is there, not how they feel about it. Draw the picture with words; keep opinions out unless specifically invited.

Finally, when the candidate asks for clarification, do what you would train them to do: repeat the statement, or paraphrase and simplify it. Model the behaviour you want from them.

### 6. How to Give Feedback That Trains

The feedback a teacher gives after a practice assessment is one of the strongest shapers of a candidate's next performance. Two principles matter above all.

**Draw the reflection out of the candidate first.** Open the feedback with their reading of their own performance, not yours. Use questions like:

- "What do you think?"
- "What area do you think you can improve?"
- "How do you feel?"
- "Where do you think there might be an issue?"

You will often find the candidate names the right weakness themselves, which makes the work that follows theirs rather than yours.

**Be blurry. Never give a number.** Resist the urge to say "that was a Level 3" or "you're a 4 today." Numbers freeze the learner. They also pre-empt the real examiner, who may read the sample differently. Describe the behaviour you heard — what worked, what faltered, what pattern repeated — and leave the number out. Learners who train against behaviours improve; learners who train against numbers plateau at the number they were last told.

### 7. What You Should Build Into Every Lesson

Working back from what the examiner is looking for, there are seven habits that deserve a place in every lesson you plan.

- **Clarification routines.** Say again, confirm, request repetition, request rephrasing. These should become automatic, not optional — the candidate must leave the course asking rather than guessing.
- **Paraphrasing drills.** When the exact word fails, there is always another way to say the thing. Train it with vocabulary tasks where the key word is removed.
- **Describe-the-picture practice.** Objective, structured, factual. No opinions unless asked. Order of description: what, where, who, state.
- **Three-to-four-sentence answer practice.** Short-answer habits are hard to break in the exam room. Build the longer-answer habit now, with simple scaffolds: statement, reason, example.
- **Unexpected-turn tasks.** Role-plays where the script breaks halfway through. The candidate who can keep going here is the candidate who passes.
- **Listening under pressure.** Faster speech, varied accents, overlapping noise. The real radio is not a studio recording.
- **Reflective closes.** Finish each practice with the candidate telling you what they thought went well and what they would do differently. Train the reflection muscle — it is the muscle that improves them between lessons.

### 8. The Shape of the Exam

The exam is designed to mimic real life as closely as a 20-minute conversation can. The examiner conducts a holistic evaluation — the six skills are rated separately, but what is being judged is the candidate's overall ability to communicate.

A few things to brief your candidates on before they walk in:

- The structure is the same whether the audio comes first or the picture does; what varies is the content, not the shape of the test.
- The exam has to feel like a natural daily conversation, not a performance. Candidates who try to perform tend to stiffen; candidates who treat it as a conversation score better.
- Spontaneous reaction is what is being analysed. It is more important to react appropriately than to react perfectly. Being too afraid to ask for clarification is a worse sign than asking for it clearly.
- It is okay not to understand. It is not okay to pretend you did.
- Visual feedback does not count. Nodding and mimicking are not language. If the candidate relies on these, the examiner will come back verbally — and that moment will be rated.

### 9. The Legal Backdrop (Short Version)

Teachers do not need to memorise every circular, but a handful of acronyms and rules keep coming up and should be familiar.

- **ICAO** — International Civil Aviation Organization. The source of the Language Proficiency Requirements.
- **EASA** — European Union Aviation Safety Agency. The European regulator that transposes the ICAO LPRs into EU law.
- **CAA** — the national Civil Aviation Authority in each state.
- **Doc 9835** — ICAO's implementation manual: how the proficiency requirements should be put into practice, trained and tested.
- **Circular 318** — ICAO's language testing criteria for global harmonisation. What a good aviation language test looks like.
- **Circular 323** — ICAO's aviation English training guide. How to train, not how to test.
- **FCL.055** — the European rule (Part-FCL, Regulation (EU) No 1178/2011) that makes the language proficiency endorsement a condition of exercising a pilot licence.

Two practical consequences flow from all of this. First, nobody flies internationally without an ICAO language test — the endorsement is the ticket. Second, Levels 1, 2 and 3 do not pass; they do not fly. Air traffic controllers face the same regime through Regulation (EU) 2015/340.

Finally, a vocabulary note worth passing on to candidates, because it recurs in the material they will encounter: an *incident* is an occurrence in which nobody dies; an *accident* is one in which people do. The whole language proficiency regime exists because the line between the two has, more than once, been a word misheard or a question not asked.

### 10. One Sentence to End On

If a teacher took only one thing from this guide into their next lesson, it would be this: train the behaviour you want to see in the exam, and train it until it is automatic. The candidate who asks when unsure, keeps the channel open under pressure, structures a short answer with a reason and an example, and comes back from a stumble without going silent — that candidate is the one the examiner is looking for. Everything else is detail.

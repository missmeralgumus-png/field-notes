If you have spent your career teaching General English, Business English, or even English for Specific Purposes in fields like tourism or finance, moving into aviation English can feel like stepping into an entirely different world. The usual markers of a "good" English learner — grammatical polish, a wide idiomatic range, a near-native accent — are suddenly beside the point. What matters instead is whether a pilot or air traffic controller can, under pressure, make themselves clearly understood to someone they cannot see, in a situation that may have no precedent in their training, when any ambiguity could put lives at risk.

This article is an orientation to the framework that governs how aviation professionals' English is defined, trained, and tested worldwide. It draws on four of the key documents any aviation English teacher needs to recognise: ICAO Doc 9835 (Manual on the Implementation of ICAO Language Proficiency Requirements, 2nd ed., 2010), ICAO Circular 323 (Guidelines for Aviation English Training Programmes), ICAO Circular 318 (Language Testing Criteria for Global Harmonization), and, from the European regulatory side, Commission Regulation (EU) No 1178/2011 (Part-FCL, for pilots) and Commission Regulation (EU) 2015/340 (for air traffic controllers).

### 1. Why ICAO Created the Requirements

The decision to address language proficiency for pilots and controllers was first taken by the 32nd Session of the ICAO Assembly in September 1998, following a series of fatal accidents in which insufficient English language proficiency on the part of flight crew or controllers was identified as a causal or contributing factor. Doc 9835 records that over 800 people lost their lives in three major accidents — a collision on the ground, an accident involving fuel exhaustion, and a controlled flight into terrain — where accident investigators identified insufficient English language proficiency as a common thread. The concern was sharpened by a 1996 mid-air collision in which 349 passengers and crew lost their lives.

Doc 9835 identifies three distinct ways in which language can contribute to accidents and incidents: incorrect use of standardised phraseology; lack of plain language proficiency; and the use of more than one language in the same airspace, which erodes the situational awareness of flight crews who do not understand all the languages in use.

In March 2003, following work by the Proficiency Requirements in Common English Study Group (PRICESG), the ICAO Council adopted amendments to Annexes 1, 6, 10 and 11 and to the PANS-ATM, introducing the Language Proficiency Requirements (LPRs). As of 5 March 2008, the ability to speak and understand the language used for radiotelephony is formally required of pilots, air traffic controllers and aeronautical station operators, evaluated against the ICAO rating scale and the holistic descriptors, with a minimum of ICAO Operational Level 4.

For ESL teachers, the first big shift to internalise is this: training is no longer ad hoc or budget-driven. It is driven by a specific, measurable, safety-critical operational standard. European regulators have reinforced the same message. Commission Regulation (EU) 2015/340 opens its justification by stating that "poor communication is often a significant contributing factor in incidents and accidents" and that language proficiency requirements for air traffic controllers are therefore set "based on the requirements adopted by the International Civil Aviation Organization."

### 2. The Nature of the Language

ICAO Doc 9835 is careful to distinguish the full spectrum of aviation language use. The LPRs do not target aviation language in general — which covers everything from aircraft maintenance to passenger care — but a very specific subcategory: aeronautical radiotelephony communications between pilots and controllers. Within that subcategory, two kinds of language coexist.

**Standardised phraseology** is the clipped, formulaic code of radiotelephony ("Cleared visual approach runway one three left", "Descend flight level two eight zero"). Doc 9835 describes it as a true sub-language, built on a reduced vocabulary of around 400 words in which each word has a precise, often aviation-exclusive meaning, with sentences shortened by the deletion of function words. Around half of all sentences are imperative or passive. It is designed for routine situations and taught as an operational discipline by qualified operational instructors, not by language teachers.

Phraseology works. But it does not cover everything. A frequently cited study (Mell, 1992) found that roughly 70 per cent of real en-route radiotelephony speech acts, uttered by native and non-native speakers alike, did not comply with prescribed ICAO phraseology. No set of phraseologies can fully describe every circumstance that arises.

**Plain language** is defined in ICAO documentation as "the spontaneous, creative and non-coded use of a given natural language." In radiotelephony, Annex 10, Volume II specifies that plain language shall be used "only when standardised phraseology cannot serve an intended transmission." This is the territory the LPRs are concerned with, and it is the territory your teaching must prepare learners for.

Doc 9835 emphasises that the need for plain language arises not only in dramatic emergencies but throughout everyday operations: a pilot reporting a sick passenger; a controller explaining an unusual holding pattern; a crew describing debris on a taxiway; a request for permission that falls outside any script ("Can we keep high speed?" or "Who's ahead? Us or the Air Europe?" — both drawn from real transcripts in Doc 9835). Doc 9835 also notes the phenomenon of code-switching, where speakers alternate between phraseology and plain language within a single exchange, and the challenges of bilingual airspace.

### 3. The Six Skills and the Six Levels

The ICAO rating scale assesses six language skills:

- **Pronunciation** — intelligibility to the international aeronautical community
- **Structure** — control of basic and (at higher levels) complex grammatical patterns
- **Vocabulary** — range, accuracy, and the ability to paraphrase when a word fails
- **Fluency** — tempo, pausing, and the ability to produce stretches of spontaneous speech
- **Comprehension** — understanding on routine topics and when something unexpected happens
- **Interactions** — quality and timing of responses, and the ability to recognise and resolve misunderstandings

Doc 9835 presents these not as a flat list but as a pyramid. Structure, Vocabulary and Pronunciation are the linguistic foundations at the base; Fluency and Comprehension are the performance skills built on them; Interaction sits at the apex, drawing on all the others. Each level of the pyramid depends on the levels below.

Each skill is rated on a six-point scale, from Level 1 (Pre-Elementary) to Level 6 (Expert). Level 4 (Operational) is the minimum acceptable for radiotelephony. Doc 9835 is explicit that Level 4 was constructed precisely because the recognition of error patterns at that threshold corresponds to what is "considered to be the minimum level acceptable to ensure safe operations."

**The lowest-score rule.** One rule governs how the final rating is calculated, and ESL teachers often find it counterintuitive: the final score is the lowest score across the six skills. There is no averaging. A pilot who scores Level 5 on five skills and Level 3 on comprehension is rated Level 3 overall. Level 4 was deliberately set as the safest minimum across all six skills, because a weakness in any one of them can be the point at which safety breaks down.

**How often it must be re-demonstrated.** ICAO treats language proficiency as a career-long requirement. Doc 9835 notes that language loss is a well-documented phenomenon in users who do not practise regularly, so proficiency must be re-evaluated periodically. European regulation gives this precise shape. Under both Part-FCL (for pilots) and Regulation (EU) 2015/340 (for controllers), the language proficiency endorsement is valid for 4 years at Level 4, 6 years at Level 5, and does not require re-evaluation at Level 6, provided the Level 6 assessment has been properly documented.

### 4. What a Level 4 Speaker Actually Sounds Like

Level 4 is often misunderstood by teachers coming from a general EFL background, because our instinct is to read the descriptors against a native-speaker ideal. The ICAO scale is calibrated against operational safety, not stylistic polish.

A Level 4 speaker has first-language influence in their pronunciation but is still intelligible to international listeners. They control basic grammatical structures and attempt more complex ones, with errors that mainly appear under pressure but rarely obscure meaning. Their vocabulary is sufficient for common, concrete, work-related topics, and they can usually paraphrase when a precise word fails them. They produce speech at an appropriate tempo, with occasional stumbles when moving from rehearsed to spontaneous speech — but these stumbles do not prevent communication. Their comprehension is reliable on routine content and holds up, perhaps more slowly, when something unexpected arises. And they manage interactions well, initiating and sustaining exchanges and checking or clarifying when something is unclear.

This is not an elegant speaker. This is a safe and operationally effective speaker. The distinction matters.

### 5. What ICAO Does Not Expect

Equally important for ESL teachers is understanding what is not required.

- **Native-like pronunciation is not the goal.** Doc 9835 states that in the context of English as a lingua franca — where non-native users outnumber native users roughly three to one — native-speaker norms are "no longer appropriate" as the model, and that EIL research (Jenkins, 2000) treats native-like pronunciation as "not only unlikely but also unnecessary." What matters is mutually comprehensible pronunciation built around the lingua-franca "core phonology": long/short vowel distinctions, correct placement of nuclear stress, clear tone boundaries, and avoidance of over-reduced consonant clusters.
- **Grammatical perfection is not the criterion.** Errors that do not interfere with meaning are tolerated at every level. What matters is whether an error damages the message, not how "basic" or "advanced" the structure was.
- **Idiomatic sophistication is not expected below Level 6.** Doc 9835 is explicit that the native-speaker expectation — that any native speaker will perform at the highest level in all areas — "is not founded on real observations." Native speakers with heavy regional accents, specialist jargon, or inattentive delivery can themselves fail to meet the LPRs, and must accommodate to less proficient interlocutors.
- **Reading, writing and grammar-on-paper are not tested.** Circular 318 is categorical: testing reading ability, English grammar in isolation, or vocabulary items out of context "is not consistent with ICAO requirements."

### 6. What ICAO Does Expect

The holistic descriptors in Annex 1 of the Chicago Convention (reproduced in Doc 9835 and taken into European law in both Part-FCL and Regulation 2015/340) state that proficient speakers shall be able to:

- communicate effectively in voice-only (radiotelephone) and face-to-face situations;
- communicate on common, concrete, and work-related topics with accuracy and clarity;
- use appropriate communicative strategies to exchange messages and to recognise and resolve misunderstandings in a general or work-related context;
- handle successfully, and with relative ease, the linguistic challenges presented by a complication or unexpected turn of events within the context of a routine work situation or communicative task with which they are otherwise familiar;
- use a dialect or accent that is intelligible to the aeronautical community.

Read those five points carefully. Notice what is prized: effectiveness, clarity, strategy, resilience under unexpected pressure, and intelligibility. Nothing about elegance, native-speaker norms, or literary vocabulary.

Notice too that point 4 — handling complications and unexpected events — is where the LPRs really probe a speaker. Routine performance can be carried by memorised chunks. What reveals real proficiency is what happens when the script runs out: an unexpected instruction, a passenger emergency, an unfamiliar system failure. Can the speaker still initiate? Still paraphrase? Still check, confirm, and clarify? That is the moment the assessment looks at.

### 7. The Communicative Foundations

Chapter 2 of Doc 9835 presents a view of language proficiency that goes well beyond grammar and vocabulary. Communicative competence has three strands:

- **Linguistic competence** — lexical, grammatical, semantic and phonological knowledge;
- **Sociolinguistic competence** — sensitivity to register, politeness conventions, dialect, accent and the social context in which language is used;
- **Pragmatic competence** — the strategic, discourse and functional skills needed to use language purposefully in context.

Doc 9835 also distinguishes between language learning (analytical, conscious, typical of adult classroom study) and language acquisition (unconscious, built up through meaningful use). Both have advantages. Classroom learning builds rapid accuracy but risks never producing spontaneous fluency; acquisition builds spontaneity but risks fossilisation — the stabilising of errors at a plateau below the proficiency the community requires. Doc 9835 warns that fossilisation is a particular risk in pronunciation, which matters because pronunciation at Level 4 is one of the areas where candidates most often fall below standard.

The implication for teaching is that an aviation English programme needs to mobilise both processes: structured learning for accuracy, and meaningful, context-rich use for spontaneous fluency.

### 8. Communicative Functions: What Pilots and Controllers Actually Do with Language

Chapter 3 of Doc 9835 and its Appendix B give an inventory of the dominant themes, topics, communicative functions and exchange structures of radiotelephony communications. The communicative functions — the purposes speakers use language for — are grouped into four categories:

- **Triggering actions** — the core of pilot-controller communication (giving instructions, requesting clearance, acknowledging);
- **Sharing information** — the enabling layer that makes action possible (reporting, describing, confirming);
- **Managing the pilot-controller relationship** — politeness, repair, mitigation;
- **Managing the dialogue** — turn-taking, signalling non-understanding, closing an exchange.

The dominant themes range widely across weather, numbers, geography, health, modality (obligation, probability, possibility), rules and infringement, problems and malfunctions, and so on. Doc 9835 explicitly recommends that language course planners and teachers use the Appendix B checklists to formulate linguistically appropriate training and testing objectives.

### 9. How ICAO Expects Testing to Work (Circular 318)

Circular 318 (Language Testing Criteria for Global Harmonization, 2009) sets out the recommended criteria for anyone developing or selecting aviation language tests. The material is now integrated into Chapter 6 of the second edition of Doc 9835, but Circular 318 remains the detailed reference for test design and administration.

Four principles frame everything else:

- **Validity** — the test measures what it claims to measure (aviation-relevant spoken and listening proficiency, assessed against the ICAO rating scale and holistic descriptors).
- **Reliability** — the test produces consistent results across different candidates, raters, forms and administrations.
- **Practicality** — the balance between the resources a test needs and the resources available to run it.
- **Washback** — the influence of the test on the teaching and learning that precedes it. Circular 318 is explicit that a valid, communicative test produces positive washback (encouraging real proficiency-building work), while a test built on discrete grammar or vocabulary items drives learners away from genuine communicative skill.

Circular 318 also distinguishes direct testing (face-to-face or telephonic interaction with a live interlocutor) from semi-direct testing (pre-recorded prompts). Semi-direct tests gain standardisation but may be constrained in their ability to assess the interaction descriptors of the rating scale, especially the resolution of unexpected turns.

On Level 6, Circular 318 takes a notable position: because Level 6 descriptors describe features beyond the work-related context of lower levels, formal Level 6 assessment may be carried out by a trained and qualified rater (not necessarily a language testing specialist) through documented assessment "events" rather than full specialised tests. Even so, documented evidence is essential — informal validation of Level 6 is not recommended, since a Level 6 result exempts the holder from any further language proficiency re-evaluation for the rest of their career.

### 10. How ICAO Expects Training to Work (Circular 323)

Circular 323 (Guidelines for Aviation English Training Programmes) is the ICAO reference most directly aimed at trainers and training providers. Its central argument is that aviation English should be taught through content-based language learning (CBLT): language learnt in and through the operational content that pilots and controllers actually deal with, rather than through generic topics onto which aviation vocabulary is grafted.

This matters because the LPRs are an exercise in language for specific purposes (LSP). Doc 9835 points out that aeronautical radiotelephony is a specialised sub-category of aviation language, and that its lexicon, grammar and functions are shaped by what pilots and controllers do. Training that treats aviation only as a theme misses the point; training that treats it as the organising principle of the syllabus aligns with the test construct and produces positive washback.

### 11. The Regulatory Side: Pilots and Controllers Separately

In European law, pilots and air traffic controllers are regulated by two different instruments, though both carry the ICAO LPRs over faithfully.

For pilots, the LPRs sit in Commission Regulation (EU) No 1178/2011 (Part-FCL), with FCL.055 as the language proficiency rule and Appendix 2 reproducing the full ICAO rating scale. Pilots exercising the privileges of a licence must demonstrate at least Operational Level 4 in the language used for radiotelephony.

For air traffic controllers, the LPRs sit in Commission Regulation (EU) 2015/340, specifically ATCO.B.030 (language proficiency endorsement), ATCO.B.035 (validity), ATCO.B.040 (assessment) and ATCO.B.045 (language training), with Appendix 1 of Annex I reproducing the ICAO rating scale. Controllers must demonstrate at least Operational Level 4 in English, and in any other language required by the air navigation service provider for the sector they work in.

For an ESL teacher, the practical point is that the standard is the same, but the operational world of your learners is not. Pilots and controllers approach communication from different perspectives — controllers from the perspective of overall traffic management in an airspace, pilots from the perspective of their own flight — and Doc 9835 observes that this divergence is itself one of the reasons plain language is needed. Training content and example scenarios should recognise this difference rather than blur the two.

### 12. Implications for Your Teaching

Even without going into methodology in depth, a few implications follow directly from what ICAO and EASA expect:

- **The focus is overwhelmingly oral.** Doc 9835 Chapter 3 is categorical: aeronautical radiotelephony requires speaking and listening, not reading and writing. Testing is of spoken and listening ability only.
- **Context must be operationally relevant.** Content about weather, approaches, separation, system failures, abnormal procedures and emergencies does more than motivate — it builds the specific vocabulary and functions that will be needed when they count. Generic conversational English is not enough.
- **Communicative functions matter as much as topics.** Build activities around the four function categories (triggering actions, sharing information, managing the relationship, managing the dialogue), not just around themes.
- **Paraphrasing is a core skill, not a fallback.** A learner who can only produce the exact word they memorised is operationally fragile. Build paraphrasing in from the start.
- **Prepare for the unexpected.** Build in activities that force learners off-script — role plays with unanticipated turns, listening tasks with complications introduced partway through, problem-solving scenarios where the "right" answer has to be negotiated.
- **Mobilise both learning and acquisition.** Pair focused accuracy work with meaningful, context-rich use. Fossilised errors — especially in pronunciation — are hard to shift later.
- **Train for proficiency, not for the test.** Circular 318 identifies this precisely as a washback issue: a learner who crams for discrete-point items will fail the communicative demands of the real job even if they pass the paper.

### 13. A Final Word on the Stakes

In most ESL contexts, if your learner struggles for a word in a meeting or writes an awkward email, the consequences are mild embarrassment. In aviation, a misunderstood instruction, an ambiguous report, or a silence where a question should have been can contribute to loss of life. Doc 9835 cites two illustrative examples drawn from accident records:

> Controller: "Descend two four zero zero feet." — where the similarity between "two" and "to" led the pilot to understand 400 feet instead of 2,400 feet. The aircraft crashed into high ground.

> Pilot: "We are at take-off." — where the controller understood the pilot to be waiting in position, whereas the aircraft had begun to accelerate along the runway. It collided with another aircraft in fog.

This is the responsibility you take on when you teach aviation English. The ICAO Language Proficiency Requirements exist because the industry recognised that language is, as it is sometimes put, the final safety net. Your job is to help weave that net tightly enough to hold.

The rest of your preparation — the methodology, the materials, the trainer training — flows from this starting point. But the starting point itself is simple: understand that your learners are not learning English for its own sake. They are learning it so that, on a day they hope never to see, the words they need will be there.

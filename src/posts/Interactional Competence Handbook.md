# Interactional Competence

What it is, what the research says about teaching it, and a ready-to-use lesson cycle, activity bank, and assessment rubric — built for teachers who want adults to *do things with others* in the language, not just know the language.

Synthesised from 13 sources Kramsch 1986 → Pacheco et al. 2023 CEFR A2–C1 Share & adapt freely

Part 1 — The construct

## What interactional competence actually is

Interactional competence (IC) is not what a single person knows. It is what people build together, turn by turn, in a particular kind of talk. That one shift — from a property of the individual to an achievement of the participants — changes everything about how it is taught and tested.

### From knowledge to co-construction

The idea runs through a clear genealogy. Chomsky's (1965) *competence* was an individual's idealised knowledge of well-formed sentences. Hymes (1972) broke that open with *communicative competence*: "there are rules of use without which the rules of grammar are useless" — knowing what is possible, feasible, appropriate, and actually done. Canale & Swain (1980) made this teachable (grammatical, sociolinguistic, discourse, strategic components) but still treated it as something inside one learner.

Kramsch's (1986) essay is the hinge. She argued that oral-proficiency ratings measured what was controllable and testable in *one* speaker, while real communication is "a two-way negotiative effort" — the construction of a shared "sphere of inter-subjectivity." Every later author in this field cites that essay as the origin point. Young (2011) states it plainly: IC is the **pragmatic relationship between participants' resources and the contexts in which they are used**, employed "mutually and reciprocally." It is co-constructed, distributed across the people talking, and specific to the practice they are engaged in.

The root idea: intersubjectivity

Successful interaction reduces each party's uncertainty about the other's intentions. The lineage is old — Husserl's phenomenology → Trevarthen's (1979) two-month-old infants coordinating with a parent → Wells's (1981) triangular "sender–receiver–situation" → Kramsch's (1986) L2 classroom. When a pilot says *"confirm,"* or a learner recycles a partner's wording to show they followed, they are doing the same job: making the two minds meet.

### The resources learners draw on

Young (2011) catalogues IC as a bundle of resources participants deploy and recognise in each other (building on He & Young, 1998). They fall into three groups:

#### Identity resources

The participation framework — who may speak, who is addressed, who overhears, and the social roles the talk makes relevant (boss/employee, expert/novice).

#### Linguistic resources

Register and the practice's own ways of meaning — the specific words, structures, and formulae that "belong" to this kind of encounter.

#### Interactional resources

The machinery of talk: speech acts, turn-taking, repair, and the signalling of boundaries (how talk opens and closes).

### The four organisations: the machinery of talk

When researchers look closely at how IC develops, they track four systems borrowed from Conversation Analysis (Sacks, Schegloff & Jefferson, 1974; applied to L2 development by Pekarek Doehler & Pochon-Berger, 2015). These are the backbone of the activity bank in Part 4, so they are worth holding onto:

Turn-taking Sequence organisation Repair Preference organisation

#### Turn-taking

How the floor is taken, held, and handed over — self-selecting at the right moment, timing an entry to a transition point, yielding smoothly, and using listener tokens (*mhm, right*) to keep the other going.

#### Sequence organisation

How actions are built across turns and projected ahead — openings, pre-sequences, story prefaces, topic launches and shifts — so a co-participant can recognise what is coming and fit their turn to it.

#### Repair

How trouble in speaking, hearing, or understanding is handled — initiating repair, soliciting help, and (the developmental goal) self-repairing and try-marking in ways that preserve the flow of talk.

#### Preference organisation

How dispreferred actions — above all disagreement — get softened: delays, hedges, accounts, and "yes-but" architecture, rather than a bald turn-initial *"No."*

### How IC develops — and why it isn't a straight line

The longitudinal studies deliver two findings that should reshape anyone's expectations in the classroom.

**It is recalibrated, not transferred, and not unilinear.** Adults arrive with a fully working L1 interactional system; L2 development is the *reworking* of those methods for new resources and norms, not building from zero. And it is bumpy. In Cekaite's (2007) classic case, a seven-year-old in a Swedish immersion class moved through three phases — *silent* (relying on being nominated), then *loud and mistimed* (winning turns by volume, blunt *"no"*-disagreements, self-selecting at the wrong moment), then *skilful* (precision-timed entries, mitigated disagreement with accounts). The same learner looked less competent in phase two than phase one. Regression is often reorganisation.

**What changes is often the purpose a form serves, not the form itself.** Pekarek Doehler & Pochon-Berger (2015) track learners who *already had* a word like *but / mais* early on — using it to grab a turn — before later repurposing it to mitigate disagreement and tie back to a prior speaker's wording ("format-tying"). Development is the **diversification of methods** that lets a learner do recipient design: fitting the turn to *this* listener, in *this* context. This is why "learn more grammar → interact better" is the wrong model.

**Novice:** No. I don't agree. **Skilled:** Yeah, no, I see what you mean about the cost — but the thing is, if we wait we lose the slot. ↑ same disagreement; the second ties back, hedges, and accounts for itself

### Why this matters — Kramsch's warning

Pragmatic slips are more dangerous than grammatical ones, because listeners don't hear them as *language* errors. A mistimed interruption or an unmitigated refusal isn't filed as "still learning" — it is reattributed to the speaker's character: rude, evasive, cold. Thomas (1983) called this **pragmatic failure**. In safety-critical settings the stakes are literal: Pacheco et al.'s (2023) aviation work shows pilots and controllers leaving fixed phraseology for spontaneous "plain English" in emergencies, where confirmation and clarification routines — repair and preference organisation under pressure — keep two non-native speakers understanding each other. Interactional competence is where a learner's humanity is read. That is the case for teaching it directly.

Part 2 — The pedagogy

## How to teach it, and why these choices work

You cannot teach "co-construction" head-on. You can teach the linguistic resources and the social sensitivity that let a learner take part in it. The move that makes IC teachable is to treat it as **form–function–context mapping, practised in interaction.**

### The evidence is unusually clear

Derakhshan & Shakki's (2023) meta-analysis (234 effect sizes across requests, apologies, and refusals) and Taguchi & Kim's (2018) synthesis of 58 intervention studies point the same way. The headline numbers — field benchmarks are *d*≈0.60 small, 1.00 medium, 1.40 large:

⚠ The measurement catch — read before you assess

The biggest effects came from **written discourse-completion tasks** (the most controlled measure) and were *smaller* for role-plays (free, interactive). Controlled tasks flatter your results. If your real goal is IC, you have to assess with interactive tasks even though the numbers look more modest — those numbers are simply more honest. (See Part 5.)

### The five features every effective lesson assembles

Across the 58 studies (Taguchi & Kim, 2018), the instruction that worked combined the same five ingredients. They are the DNA of the lesson cycle in Part 3:

1. **Input** — authentic pragmatic input with the target forms *in context* (dialogues, film/TV clips, real transcripts, corpus concordance lines). Present in 90 of 91 conditions: non-negotiable.
2. **Metapragmatic information** — explicit explanation of the form, its function, and the contextual factors that govern it. The single highest-yield lever.
3. **Consciousness-raising** — learners *notice* the form–function–context link themselves, through comparison and appropriateness judgement.
4. **Production** — structured practice moving toward freer, interactive use.
5. **Feedback** — on their pragmatic use, not just their grammar.

### Two dimensions to plan with

Thomas's (1983) distinction underlies every activity here. **Pragmalinguistics** is the resource — the actual phrases (*I was wondering if…, Sorry to jump in, That reminds me…*). **Sociopragmatics** is knowing which resource fits the situation, read off four variables you should name for learners every time:

#### Social status

Boss ↔ employee, teacher ↔ student, expert ↔ client. Who outranks whom, and does the talk make that relevant?

#### Social / psychological distance

Strangers, acquaintances, close friends, family. How well do they know each other?

#### Imposition / weight of the act

Borrowing a pen vs. asking for a week off. How costly or face-threatening is what's being done?

#### Setting

A meeting, a corridor, a phone call, a message thread. Where is it happening, and how formal is that place?

### A menu of approaches — all evidence-backed

There is no single "best" method; the right one depends on your learners and goals (Barón et al., 2023). These are the approaches that have shown positive effects, and you will mix them within a course:

- **Deductive** — give the rule, then practise. **Inductive** — let learners notice the pattern, then surface the rule. (Both work; the meta-analysis leans explicit.)
- **Task-supported (TSLT)** — explain first, then run a meaningful task. The explicit-friendly default for IC, and the spine of Part 3.
- **Task-based (TBLT)** — task first, explanation after, with feedback. A genuine "task" has a real outcome, an information gap, and makes learners rely on their own resources.
- **Concept-based** — present the whole practice as a meaningful system from the start (its form–function–context logic), rather than drilling fragments.
- **Usage-based** — learn forms through repeated meaningful use with others.
- **CA-based** — teach explicitly that moves are co-built across turns: a request may take several mitigating turns, and the other person's uptake shapes what comes next.

The adult advantage

Adults bring a mature L1 pragmatic system and strong metalinguistic awareness — so lean on contrast ("how would you do this in your language? does it work the same way?") and on real relevance. The far end of the production scale is where adults thrive: Louw, Derwing & Abbott (2010) had adult immigrants do **mock job interviews with real recruiters**, then watch their own recordings and compare them to a model. Authentic players, real stakes, a built-in noticing loop. That is the template for adult IC work.

Part 3 — The template

## The lesson cycle

One reusable arc for teaching any interactional resource. Pick *one* resource per cycle (not a vague "fluency"), run these seven steps, and reuse with the next resource. Each step realises one of the five features from Part 2.

1

#### Needs & target situation

Find out which real interactions your adults struggle with — meetings, phone calls, disagreeing with a manager, small talk. Choose one resource and one situation to build the cycle around.

Needs analysis · sets status / distance / imposition / setting

2

#### Input & noticing

Give authentic input (a clip, a real transcript, concordance lines) and have learners *find* the resource — underline the topic-shifts, mark where the listener backchannels, spot the mitigation.

Feature 1 input + Feature 3 consciousness-raising

3

#### Explicit metapragmatics

State the form, its function, and the contextual conditions. "We use *Sorry to interrupt, but…* to take the floor when the other person outranks us or we don't know them well." Don't skip this — it's the highest-yield step.

Feature 2 metapragmatic information

4

#### Controlled production

Structured practice of the form: matching, gap-fill, redesign-for-this-person, short scripted exchanges. Low stakes, high repetition, fast feedback.

Feature 4 production (structured)

5

#### Freer interactive task

An *open* role-play or real-world task where the other person's response isn't scripted — so co-construction can actually happen. This is the point of the whole cycle.

Feature 4 production (free) · the IC payoff

6

#### Reflection

Observers (or a recording) review against a focused sheet: *Did they show interest? Involve others? Was their directness about right? How did they change topic? What would you have said differently — and how in your own language?*

Consciousness-raising · "prosaics of interaction" (Hall, 1999)

7

#### Feedback & assessment

Feedback on the pragmatics, then assess with the interactive task — scored on a normed rubric (Part 5), accepting honest numbers over inflated discrete-task ones. Treat each cycle as action research: pre-test, teach, observe, refine.

Feature 5 feedback + assessment

Part 4 — The activities

## Activity bank, by interactional resource

Organised around the four CA organisations plus boundaries and the prosodic / recipient-design layer. Each activity names a level, a focus, a quick procedure, an adult adaptation, and its source. Slot any of them into step 2, 4, or 5 of the cycle.

### ● Turn-taking & floor management

taking, holding, yielding the floor · interrupting · backchannelling

Jump in politelyB1+ · 50m

Give a phrase bank for interrupting (*May I jump in? · Sorry to interrupt, but… · Before you go on…*) and for showing interest (*mhm · right · good point · really?*). Run a group discussion; monitor who holds the floor; reshuffle groups and repeat.

**Adult:** a real meeting — agreeing project priorities or a budget split.

Barón et al. (2023), C8 "Who needs technology?"

Backchannel radarA2+ · 30m

Play authentic audio; learners mark every point where the listener signals they're still there. Then do a 3-minute "active listening" pair task where the listener may only backchannel — no full turns.

**Adult:** a colleague venting about a delayed deliverable; practise listening without hijacking.

CA turn-taking · Pekarek Doehler & Pochon-Berger (2015)

### ● Sequence & topic management

launching & shifting topics · projecting what's coming · story prefaces

Changing the subjectB1+ · 40m

Learners mine short dialogues for topic-shift markers (*by the way · that reminds me · going back to what we were saying · anyway*), classify return-vs-new-topic, then write and perform their own two exchanges using at least one each.

**Adult:** move from workplace small talk into the actual ask.

Barón et al. (2023), C1 "Changing the subject"

Anchor the storyB1+ · 35m

Teach how to *launch* a report so the listener can follow — anchor in time, preview the point (*So this happened last week… you won't believe it…*). Learners record a 60-second "something that happened to me" with a proper preface.

**Adult:** a status update that opens with the headline, not the chronology.

Pekarek Doehler & Pochon-Berger (2015, story-openings)

### ● Repair & negotiation of meaning

signalling trouble · soliciting help · self-repair & try-marking

Say that again?A2+ · 40m

Build a ladder of repair-initiators from blunt to smooth: *Sorry? → Sorry, do you mean…? → …the Tuesday invoice? (try-marking) → paraphrase.* Run an information-gap task that forces repair, then push learners up the ladder.

**Adult:** clarifying an ambiguous instruction from a client without sounding incompetent.

CA repair · Pekarek Doehler & Pochon-Berger (2015)

Confirm & read-backB1+ · 40m

Teach confirmation / clarification routines (*So just to confirm… · Can you confirm…? · Let me make sure I've got this*). High-stakes information-gap: one detail wrong causes a visible failure, so read-back matters.

**Adult:** taking a phone order, booking, or handover where errors cost.

Pacheco et al. (2023, aviation confirm/clarify)

### ● Preference & (dis)agreement

softening dispreferred actions · hedges · accounts · format-tying

Disagree wellB1+ · 45m

Move learners from bald *"No, I disagree"* to a "yes-but" architecture with a hedge and an account, and show format-tying (recycle the other person's words). Decision task where they must reach consensus.

**Adult:** disagreeing with a manager's plan without damaging the relationship (use the boss / new-employee / consultant role cards).

Barón et al. (2023, agree/disagree) + Pekarek Doehler & Pochon-Berger (2015)

Say it to…B1+ · 25m

Same message, redesigned for different recipients: *your best friend · your boss · a stranger · a client.* Partners guess who's being addressed. Pure recipient design and directness calibration.

**Adult:** "You spilled coffee on them — apologise" to each person on the list.

Barón et al. (2023), C5 "Say it to…"

### ● Openings, closings & boundaries

getting into and out of talk · pre-closings · signalling the edges

In and out gracefullyA2+ · 35m

Collect openers and pre-closings from authentic calls (*Right… · Anyway… · I'd better let you go · Well, it was good to…*). Learners role-play a phone call that must open *and* close properly, not just stop.

**Adult:** a professional phone call or a video-meeting that needs a clean wrap-up.

CA sequence/boundaries · Young (2011, boundary resources)

Where is it from?B2+ · 40m

Give two versions of the "same" message pitched to different audiences (a child's encyclopaedia vs. a textbook; a text to a friend vs. an email to a professor). Learners reconstruct and justify the register choices.

**Adult:** rewrite one workplace message for three audiences — a peer, a director, an external client.

Barón et al. (2023), C2 / C3 (audience & register)

### ● Prosody, embodiment & indirect meaning

intonation & stress · inferring the unsaid · integrative self-review

Say it like…A2+ · 25m

One sentence (*Where are you going?*) said as angry, scared, bored, warm. Partners guess the emotion. Makes the prosodic layer of meaning explicit and practisable.

**Adult:** deliver the same line to a client as reassuring vs. firm.

Barón et al. (2023), C4 "Say it like…"

What's the next line?A2+ · 35m

Match contexts to lines, predict replies, then unpack what each line *really* means beyond the literal (*"I have another meeting at 2:00"* = let's be quick / maybe decline). Trains uptake of implicature.

**Adult:** decode indirect pushback in work messages ("Interesting idea, let's circle back").

Barón et al. (2023), C7 "What's the next line?"

Watch yourself — the mock interviewB1+ · 2 sessions

The integrative capstone. Learners do a real-stakes simulation (job interview, performance review, client call) with an invited "real" interlocutor, are recorded, then watch themselves and compare to a model — noting turn-taking, mitigation, repair, and recipient design against the reflection sheet from step 6. Pairs an authentic goal with a built-in noticing loop.

**Adult:** use the learner's own target encounter; invite a colleague from another class as the unfamiliar interlocutor so uptake is genuinely unscripted.

Louw, Derwing & Abbott (2010) · Barón et al. (2023), C6 (role-play + observer worksheet)

Part 5 — Judging it fairly

## The assessment rubric

Assess IC with the thing itself: an **open role-play** where the interlocutor's responses aren't scripted, so co-construction can occur (Kasper & Rose, 2002). Discrete tasks (DCTs, multiple-choice) are fine for awareness and controlled form, but they can't show interactional ability — and they inflate scores (Derakhshan & Shakki, 2023). Score the interactive performance on the rubric below, which adapts the 0–6 pragmatics grid of Gilabert & Barón (2018).

| Criterion | Emerging band 1 ≈ 0–1 / 6 | Developing band 2 ≈ 2–3 / 6 | Competent band 3 ≈ 4 / 6 | Skilled band 4 ≈ 5–6 / 6 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Turn-taking & floor | Waits to be nominated; long gaps or clashing overlaps; heavy attention-getters. | Self-selects but often mistimed; takes the floor abruptly or struggles to yield. | Enters near transition points; holds and yields the floor; some backchannelling. | Precise, recipient-designed entries; holds/yields smoothly; backchannels to support the other. |
| Sequencing & topic | Turns don't project what's coming; topic jumps or stalls; hard to follow. | Some linking; topic shifts are abrupt or unmarked; thin openings. | Marks topic shifts; prefaces stories/requests so the listener can follow. | Builds actions across turns; anchors and projects; manages topic and boundaries deftly. |
| Repair & negotiation | Abandons trouble, ignores it, or switches to L1 bluntly; breakdown unresolved. | Relies on other-repair; blunt metalinguistic questions interrupt the flow. | Self-repairs; solicits help clearly; resolves most trouble. | Diversified repair — try-marking, paraphrase — that keeps talk flowing; confirms understanding. |
| Preference & mitigation | Bald, unmitigated acts (turn-initial *"No"*); no softening of face-threats. | Some hedging, inconsistently applied; mitigation often missing on heavy acts. | "Yes-but" architecture; hedges and accounts on dispreferred actions. | Calibrated mitigation with format-tying; disagreement is face-saving and well-timed. |
| Recipient design & appropriacy | One register for all; mismatched to status / distance / imposition / setting. | Register shifts attempted but uneven; some sociopragmatic mismatches. | Generally fits the interlocutor and situation; appropriate directness. | Finely tuned to this listener and context; formality, directness, and tone all fit. |
| Co-construction (holistic) | Talk feels one-sided; little uptake of the partner's contributions. | Responds to the partner but rarely builds on them; effortful. | Genuine two-way exchange; picks up and extends the partner's turns. | Jointly builds the encounter; aligns, anticipates, and leaves the partner room. |

Norming protocol — do this before you score

- **Train first.** In the source study (Gilabert & Barón, 2018), raters disagreed until they were trained — so two colleagues independently score 2–3 sample recordings, then compare and reconcile band meanings before live scoring.
- **Anchor with samples.** Keep one recorded performance per band as a reference point.
- **Score the interaction, not one turn.** Judge the whole encounter, as development shows in diversified *methods*, not single forms.

The honest caveat — built into the construct

Because performance is co-constructed, you are always partly scoring the *interlocutor* too — there is no clean way to isolate one person's contribution (McNamara, 1997; Young, 2011). Standardised oral interviews have their own unnatural shape — "the art of non-conversation" (Johnson, 2001). So keep the interlocutor role consistent across candidates, pair the rubric with learners' own reflection, and read scores as indicative, not absolute.

#### Which tool for which purpose

- **Awareness / perception** → multiple-choice DCT; appropriateness rating (learners rate messages 1–6 for fit to context).
- **Controlled production** → written or oral DCT. Fast and scalable — but remember it overstates interactional gains.
- **Interactional performance** → **open role-play** + the rubric above. The only format that actually assesses IC.

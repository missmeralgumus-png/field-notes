If you have an ICAO language test coming up, this short guide is for you. It will not teach you English — your training has done that. What it will do is explain, in plain language, what the examiner is listening for, what you are being asked to show, and how to walk into the room ready to show it. Read it a few days before the test. Read it again the night before.

### 1. First, Relax About the Wrong Things

The test is not what you may think it is. It is useful to clear a few misunderstandings straight away, because they trip up more candidates than any grammar point ever has.

- **You are not being tested on your aviation knowledge.** The examiner is not checking whether you remember a procedure, a limitation, or a regulation. They want to see how well you communicate in English about aviation. Your operational expertise is the context, not the subject.
- **You do not need to sound like a native speaker.** You never did. The test accepts an accent — any accent — as long as it is clear enough for an international listener to understand.
- **You do not need perfect grammar.** Small mistakes that do not change the meaning are fine at every level. What matters is whether the message gets through.
- **You do not need fancy vocabulary.** You need the right word for what you want to say, and a way around it if the right word does not come. A simple sentence that is clear is worth more than a complex sentence that is confused.

What is being tested is simpler, and it is something you already do every day: can you communicate — really communicate — in English, in the kind of situations pilots and controllers find themselves in?

### 2. The Six Things Being Evaluated

The examiner is listening for six specific skills. You do not need to think about them while you are talking — but it helps to know what they are.

- **Pronunciation.** Can you be understood easily? Not perfectly — easily.
- **Structure.** Are your sentences built well enough to carry your meaning?
- **Vocabulary.** Do you have the words you need, and if not, can you find another way to say what you mean?
- **Fluency.** Can you keep talking at a reasonable pace, without long silences?
- **Comprehension.** Do you understand what is being said to you — both the straightforward and the unexpected?
- **Interactions.** Can you have a real conversation — listening, responding, checking, clarifying?

> **Tip ·** Your final result will be the lowest of these six scores, not the average. One weak skill pulls everything down. This is why, in your preparation, the skill you are least comfortable with deserves the most practice — not the one you are already good at.

### 3. The Levels, in One Minute

There are six levels, from 1 (Pre-Elementary) to 6 (Expert). What you need to know:

- **Level 4 (Operational)** is the minimum to work on the radio. It means your communication is effective: not elegant, not perfect, but reliably understood. This is the real target for most candidates.
- **Level 5 (Extended)** means you handle the language with more ease — you can paraphrase comfortably, organise your ideas, attempt complex structures.
- **Level 6 (Expert)** means you are completely at home in the language — sound, easy, effortless.
- **Levels 1, 2 and 3 do not pass. They do not fly.**

The practical consequence is this: aim for Level 4 as the floor, not the ceiling. Every habit this guide recommends — asking when you do not understand, structuring your answers, paraphrasing, keeping the conversation going — is a Level 4 behaviour or higher. Habits lift levels.

### 4. The Single Most Important Thing

If you take only one thing from this guide, take this.

**If you do not understand something, ask.**

Asking is not a sign of weakness. It is one of the clearest signs of a competent aviation communicator. In the real cockpit, in the real control room, the person who checks when they are unsure is the person keeping the aircraft safe. The person who guesses is the person causing accidents.

The test rewards you for this exactly as the real world does. Say any of these, naturally, whenever you need to:

- "Say again, please."
- "Could you repeat that?"
- "I didn't catch the last part — could you say it more slowly?"
- "Did you say … ?" (and repeat back what you think you heard)
- "Just to confirm — you mean … ?"
- "Sorry, I don't understand that word. Could you explain it another way?"

What you must not do is nod, stay silent, or answer a question you did not really understand. That is the moment examiners mark down — not the moment you asked.

### 5. How to Answer a Question Well

Short answers hurt you. Not because longer is always better, but because the examiner needs enough of your English to hear what you can do. A one-word answer, or a single short sentence, gives them almost nothing to rate.

A simple structure that works almost every time:

- **Statement** — give your answer directly.
- **Reason** — say why.
- **Example or detail** — add something concrete.

Three to four sentences. That is enough to show pronunciation, structure, vocabulary, fluency, and the way you organise your ideas — five of the six skills at once.

> **Tip ·** If the question is "Do you like night flying?" the weak answer is "Yes." The strong answer is: "Yes, I enjoy it. The air is usually calmer at night, so the flight is smoother for passengers. The only part I don't enjoy is the fatigue after landing — it takes me a day to recover." Same question, five times the information.

### 6. When You Don't Have the Word

Sooner or later, in any English conversation, the word you need will not come. In the test, this is a normal moment, not a failure moment — as long as you deal with it well. The skill is called paraphrasing, and the examiner is actively looking for it.

Three ways to paraphrase around a missing word:

- **Describe what it does.** Forgot "extinguisher"? Say "the thing we use to put out fires".
- **Describe what it looks like or where it is.** Forgot "jet bridge"? Say "the passage that connects the aircraft to the terminal".
- **Give an example.** Forgot "precipitation"? Say "rain, snow, that kind of weather".

What matters is that you kept talking and the message arrived. The examiner heard your English solving a problem in real time. That is exactly the behaviour the test rewards.

### 7. The Picture Task

You will usually be asked to describe a picture. Approach it the same way every time:

- **Describe what you see — objectively.** What is in the picture, where things are, what is happening. Do not say what you feel about it unless you are asked.
- **Work from general to specific.** First the overall scene, then the main elements, then the details.
- **Use the aviation vocabulary the picture invites.** If it is an airport scene, name the aircraft type, the surface markings, the vehicles, the weather — the words you already know.
- **If you don't know the exact word, paraphrase.** You already know how.

A good description takes a minute or two of continuous speech. Short, scattered comments do not show your English. A clear, ordered account of what the picture shows does.

### 8. On the Day: What to Expect

The test is designed to feel like a natural conversation, not a performance or an interrogation. The more you can treat it as a conversation, the better your English will sound.

- **You will do most of the talking.** The interlocutor is there to guide you and to give you space to speak. Expect them to stay fairly quiet. Their silence is not a problem — it is your turn.
- **You will listen to audio of various kinds.** Different accents, different speeds, sometimes with background noise. Mirror what you would do on the radio: listen carefully, confirm what you heard, and ask if you need to.
- **You will handle something unexpected.** At some point the exchange will take a turn that is not in any script. This is deliberate. It is the examiner finding out what you do when the routine runs out. Stay engaged, keep talking, ask what you need to ask — the moment is designed to let you show your strength, not to catch you out.
- **You can take a second to think.** A short pause before you answer is normal and fine. A long silence, with no sign of effort, is not.

### 9. How to Behave in the Room

A few small things make the exchange smoother and let your English show at its best.

- **Speak clearly, not loudly.** Volume does not improve clarity. Clear vowels and a steady pace do.
- **Face the interlocutor and keep your voice level.** Mumbling into your chest is a pronunciation problem you can fix in one second.
- **Do not rely on gestures, nodding, or facial expressions to communicate.** They do not count. If you want the examiner to know you understood, say so.
- **Do not translate out loud** ("How do you say … in English?"). Just paraphrase around it.
- **Do not apologise for your English every two minutes.** One brief acknowledgement is human; repeated apologies are a distraction, and they rarely help your score.
- **Be yourself.** A warm, engaged, curious manner carries you a long way. The examiner is on your side.

### 10. Common Mistakes to Avoid

- **Memorising answers.** The examiner can hear rehearsed speech in seconds, and the test is designed to go off any script you prepared. Memorised speech signals a lower level, not a higher one.
- **Pretending you understood.** Already covered, but it is the single most damaging mistake, so it is worth repeating.
- **Giving yes/no answers.** Even if the question invites one, add a reason and a detail. You are being evaluated on your English, not on your opinion.
- **Guessing wildly at words you don't know.** Paraphrase instead. A clear description is better than a wrong technical term.
- **Rushing.** Nerves push candidates to speak faster than they can control. Slow down. A measured pace lets your pronunciation and structure settle.
- **Trying to be impressive.** Candidates who reach for the most complex structures they half-remember often collapse mid-sentence. Use the English you actually control. Clear and simple scores higher than complex and broken.

### 11. In the Week Before the Test

- **Talk aloud in English every day.** Not reading — talking. Describe your day, your flight, the weather, a photograph. Fifteen minutes a day beats three hours the night before.
- **Listen to varied English.** News, podcasts, radio exchanges from different countries. Your ear needs the range.
- **Practise clarification phrases until they are automatic.** "Say again", "I didn't catch that", "Did you say … ?" — so you don't have to think about them when you need them.
- **Describe a photo out loud** for one or two minutes, every day. Any photo.
- **Sleep well the night before.** Fatigue hurts fluency and comprehension more than any last-minute study will help them.

### 12. A Final Word

You are not walking into this test to prove that your English is perfect. You are walking in to show that you can communicate, in English, in situations that matter — clearly, calmly, and openly enough to keep the conversation moving even when it takes a turn you did not expect.

That is a skill you have been building for years, in every exchange with a controller, every briefing, every handover. The test is just one more conversation. Treat it like one. Listen first, ask when you're not sure, say what you mean in enough words to be heard, and keep the channel open.

Good luck.
